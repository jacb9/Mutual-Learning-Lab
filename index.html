Mutual Learning Lab
March (1991) · “Exploration and Exploitation in Organizational Learning” · Organization Science 2(1): 71–87

Mutual Learning Lab

March’s argument is that adaptive processes refine exploitation faster than they sustain exploration, which makes them effective in the short run and self-destructive in the long run. He makes the case with two small models. In the first, individuals and an organizational code learn from each other, and fast learning turns out to undercut what the organization can know. In the second, firms compete for first place, and the variability of performance starts to matter more than its average.

Part 1Watch an organization learn

Reality has 30 dimensions, each 1 or −1. Fifty individuals start with random beliefs (1, 0 or −1 on each dimension), and the organizational code starts out neutral (0 everywhere).

Each period, individuals adopt the code’s view on a dimension with probability p1, the socialization rate. The code, in turn, adopts the majority view of the superior group, the individuals who currently know more than the code, with a probability that rises with p2 and with how lopsided that majority is.

Neither side sees reality directly. Knowledge improves only when the code copies people who happen to be right.

What to look for. Run fast socialization (p1 = 0.9) and then slow (p1 = 0.1). With fast socialization, the matrix turns uniform within a few periods and the code stops improving early, because nobody is left who differs from it. Slow learners keep their odd beliefs long enough for the code to learn from the ones who are right.

Part 3Competition for primacy

Your organization’s performance is a draw from a normal distribution with mean x′ and variance v′. It competes against N rivals whose performance is drawn from a standard normal (mean 0, variance 1). If everyone were identical, your chance of finishing first would be 1/(N + 1).

Learning that raises the mean usually also makes performance more reliable, lowering its variance. March asks when that trade is worth it.

What to look for. With one rival, any gain in the mean helps and variance doesn’t matter. As N grows, the right-hand tail takes over: a lower mean with more variance can beat a higher mean with less, and a learning process that improves the average while cutting variability can lower the chance of coming first. How this version follows the paper, and where it departs. Parameters follow the paper: m = 30, n = 50, 80 organizations per point, equilibrium where neither individuals nor the code can change any further, and period-20 snapshots where the paper uses them. Code learning follows March’s footnote: when the superior group’s majority view on a dimension differs from the code, the code adopts it with probability 1 − (1 − p2)k, where k is the number of superior individuals holding the majority view minus the number who agree with the code. The paper calls knowledge “the proportion of reality correctly represented,” but also says random initial beliefs carry no knowledge and shows knowledge decaying to zero under turbulence, which only fits a matches-minus-contradictions measure. This lab uses that measure throughout, including to decide who is superior to the code. With it, Figures 1, 2, 4 and 5 come out with March’s shapes and close to his levels. One result does not: in Figure 3, period-20 code knowledge rises slightly as the share of fast learners grows, where March shows a slight decline, while the fast, slow and average-individual lines match. Individuals and the code update simultaneously from the previous period’s beliefs, and a departing individual’s replacement keeps the same learning rate. Figure 6 is computed by numerical integration rather than the paper’s 5,000-draw simulations, so its lines are smooth.
